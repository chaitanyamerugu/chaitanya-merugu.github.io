Part 1 · The training process

Seven stages from raw data to a deployed model

Every major lab follows roughly the same path. The bars under each stage show how much compute it uses relative to the others. Pre-training is by far the largest single expense, and serving the model to users becomes the largest ongoing one.

Data collection

Crawl the public web (e.g., Common Crawl), plus books, code, scientific papers, licensed content, and synthetic data.

Data · storage · licensing

Cleaning & tokenizing

Deduplicate, filter low-quality and toxic text, remove personal data, then split text into tokens.

Data engineers · CPU clusters

Architecture & scaling plan

Choose a Transformer design (dense or mixture-of-experts), then use small trial runs and scaling laws to pick model size and data volume.

Researchers · many small runs

Pre-training

Thousands of GPUs or TPUs learn to predict the next token across trillions of tokens. A run takes weeks to months and needs constant monitoring and recovery from hardware failures.

Compute · energy · time

Post-training & alignment

Supervised fine-tuning on example dialogues, then RLHF, DPO, or Anthropic’s Constitutional AI make the model helpful and safe.

Human labelers · compute

Evaluation & red-teaming

Run benchmark tests, safety and misuse testing, and external red teams. OpenAI spent about 6 months on this for GPT-4.

Experts · time

Deployment & inference

Compress and optimize the model, then serve it through apps and APIs. Every user request costs compute, and over a model’s lifetime those costs can exceed the cost of training it.

Ongoing compute · energy
Data phase · months
Design · weeks
Training phase · 1–4 months of compute
Launch phase · months, then ongoing

Part 2 · Resource categories

Five resources that drive cost and capability

Scaling laws show that model quality improves predictably as data and compute grow together. Every gain in capability therefore comes with a direct increase in cost.

Data

Trillions of tokens of text, code, and (for multimodal models) images, audio, and video.

Cost drivers

Licensing deals, storage, filtering pipelines, human-written and synthetic examples.

Effect on performance

Data quality and diversity matter as much as volume. Better data can stand in for raw compute.

Compute

Clusters of 2,000 to 100,000+ AI accelerators (NVIDIA GPUs, Google TPUs, AWS Trainium) linked by high-speed networks.

Cost drivers

Chips cost about $25–40K each, plus servers, networking, and the data center itself.

Effect on performance

The strongest single predictor of capability, measured in total floating-point operations (FLOP).

Energy

Each H100 GPU draws up to 700 W. A 16K-GPU cluster, with its cooling, draws tens of megawatts.

Cost drivers

Electricity, cooling, water, carbon footprint, and access to the power grid.

Effect on performance

Only about 2–6% of the cost today, but power supply increasingly limits how large a cluster can be.

Time

Pre-training takes 3 weeks to 4 months of continuous computing. The full cycle from data to release takes 6–18 months.

Cost drivers

Hardware sitting idle, failures and restarts, and delayed time to market.

Effect on performance

Longer runs allow more tokens, but competitors may ship first. Time works both as a budget and as a risk.

People

Research scientists, ML and infrastructure engineers, data annotators, and safety teams.

Cost drivers

Highly paid specialized talent, plus large numbers of human labelers for RLHF.

Effect on performance

Researcher skill decides how efficiently each dollar of compute becomes capability.

Part 4 · Model profiles

How leading labs train their models

Labs disclose very different amounts of information. Open-weight developers such as Meta and DeepSeek publish detailed training reports. OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic keep most of the details confidential.

OpenAI Few details disclosed

GPT-4 (2023)

Parameters
Not disclosed (reported ~1.8T, mixture-of-experts)
Data
~13T tokens (unofficial estimate)
Hardware
~25,000 NVIDIA A100s (unofficial estimate)
Time
~90–100 days of training, then 6 months of safety testing
Cost
$78M compute (AI Index estimate). Sam Altman said “more than $100M” in total.

Its predecessor GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B tokens) cost about $4.3M, so costs rose almost 20× in three years.

Claude (3 → 4 family)

Parameters
Not disclosed

Cost

Constitutional AI replaces some human feedback labeling with feedback generated by the AI, which shifts cost from human labor to compute.

The pattern

What the profiles show

  • Every lab follows the same pipeline: next-token pre-training, then alignment.
  • The main differences are scale and efficiency: dense vs. mixture-of-experts, numeric precision, and data quality.
  • Reported cost depends on what is counted. A $5.6M “final run” and a $100M+ “total cost” are not comparable.
  • Transparency is decreasing. Frontier labs now rarely publish parameter counts or data sizes.

Part 5 · Key takeaways

What decision-makers should remember

01

Pre-training dominates the one-time cost. The final training run is the most visible cost, but research, failed experiments, and staff can double or triple the true total.

02

Compute is the main lever for capability. Scaling laws link more FLOP and more tokens to better models, which is why cluster sizes keep growing.

03

Efficiency innovation counts as much as budget. DeepSeek-V3 and better data curation show that clever engineering can cut costs by an order of magnitude.

04

Energy is a small share of cost but a hard constraint. Power availability now determines where and how fast new clusters can be built.

05

Inference is the long-term cost. A model serving millions of users can spend more on inference over its lifetime than it cost to train.

06

Most organizations should not pre-train from scratch. For enterprises, fine-tuning or retrieval (RAG) on an existing model delivers most of the value at a tiny fraction of the cost.
